Cash and the P&L disagree

A profit-to-cash bridge whose largest line is other has not reconciled profit to cash. A gap that outlasts one operating cycle comes from an accounting policy or an estimate made under one, so the bridge should name it and who chose it.

Ashwin Kumar · 9 November 2026

A profit-to-cash bridge starts at profit, adjusts for non-cash charges and working-capital movements, and lands on operating cash flow. Every finance team produces one, and few readers question it.

The bridge has a last line, usually called other, and that line decides whether the bridge is a reconciliation. A reconciliation ties each step to a movement in a ledger account that someone can open. When other carries most of the gap, the bridge shows only that profit and cash differ by an amount everyone already knew.

When a timing difference stops being one

The usual explanation for a gap between profit and cash is timing. Revenue is booked before the customer pays, and some costs are paid before they reach the income statement. Working-capital timing differences should reverse within one operating cycle, which is the time from acquiring inputs to collecting cash from customers.

The one-cycle test should be applied to individual items, because a balance can mislead. A growing business carries a growing receivables balance, yet each invoice inside it still collects within its terms.

A receivable or unbilled balance that is still not cash after a full operating cycle has left the timing category. Profit recognised it on a basis that cash is not following, and that basis is an accounting policy or an estimate made under one. Someone chose how progress is measured on a long project, when a performance fee counts as earned, and how doubtful debts are reserved. A bridge that files the result under timing hides the policy and the person who chose it.

A hypothetical bridge, taken apart

A hypothetical business with an operating cycle of about 90 days reports operating profit of 10.0 million for a year and operating cash flow, before interest and tax, of 4.0 million. Its bridge adds back 2.0 million of depreciation. It takes off 3.0 million for higher receivables and 1.0 million for lower accrued costs, and adds 0.5 million for higher payables. The last line, other, is minus 4.5 million, which is three quarters of the 6.0 million gap.

Taking other apart at account level finds three items. The first is 0.5 million of insurance premium paid in advance for the next quarter's cover, which will be expensed within one operating cycle. That is genuine timing.

The second is 3.0 million of unbilled revenue on a fixed-price project. Revenue is recognised as cost is incurred, while billing waits for milestones, and two milestones have slipped. Recognised revenue has run ahead of billing for three consecutive quarters, and all of this year's 3.0 million increase relates to milestones that fell due more than one operating cycle ago.

The third is 1.0 million of performance fees, recognised as revenue as each service period was delivered. The fees are payable only if service targets are met. Customers have not confirmed that they were, and all of it dates from more than one operating cycle ago. Booking a fee that depends on a target before the customer confirms the target is a judgement about variable consideration, and someone has to be able to defend it.

So other holds 0.5 million of timing and 4.0 million of policy. The 3.0 million turns into cash only if the slipped milestones are met, and its size depends on a cost estimate that a named person approves. The 1.0 million is in profit because someone judged the fees earned before customers agreed them. A reader of the original bridge saw none of this, because all three sat in one line.

Why an AI-built bridge usually balances

A model asked to build this bridge is given both ends, profit and cash. Unless told otherwise, it tends to attribute what it can to named lines and put the remainder into other, so the bridge foots by construction, even when the explanation behind a line is wrong.

A balanced bridge also invites less checking than a broken one, because a reviewer tends to read a difference of zero as a sign that the work is done. The model then adds a fluent sentence for each line, other included, so the residual arrives with a reason attached that nobody has tied to an account.

People have plugged this line for decades. A model does it faster, and it writes a reason for the plug.

What the bridge should be required to show

I would hold every profit-to-cash bridge to the same standard, whoever or whatever built it. Each line should tie to a movement in a named ledger account, so a reviewer can open it and check the amount.

Nothing should be called other. Whatever does not tie should be labelled unexplained and listed by account, and the bridge should not go out until each item above a stated materiality threshold has been named.

Any item that has not turned into cash within one operating cycle should come off the timing lines. It should be shown with the policy that produced it and the name of the person who owns that policy. Where the operating cycle is the contract term, as for a long-term contractor, the test runs against the billing schedule instead: a balance past its scheduled milestone has left the timing lines. For a model, the instruction is the same: report the residual as unexplained, list the accounts behind it, and never plug.

Held to that standard, the 4.5 million of other in the hypothetical example reaches its reader as 0.5 million of timing and two named items. Each is shown with its policy and the person who owns it.
