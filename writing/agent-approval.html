What an AI agent should never be allowed to approve

An approval puts a named person between the company and a commitment, so an AI agent should hold no approval authority of its own. It can apply a rule that a named person signed.

Ashwin Kumar · 2 November 2026

A delegation-of-authority matrix answers one question for every commitment: who can say yes, and up to how much. It lists people by role and gives each a limit. Finance teams are now handing AI agents work that ends in a yes: releasing an invoice that someone else has matched to an approved order, clearing an expense claim that sits inside policy. The matrix has no row for an agent.

So in practice the agent works under the limit of whoever authorized it, and every item it clears is approved by that person, in advance, without seeing it. That may be the right design, but it should be a decision written into the matrix. An auto-match tolerance in an accounting system has cleared invoices without a row of its own for years, but a tolerance applies arithmetic that someone approved once. An agent decides whether each item fits the rule, and that call can be wrong in ways arithmetic cannot, which is why it needs a row.

What an approval is for

An approver should examine the item, but examining is not the whole control. An approval puts a named person between the company and a commitment: someone who can be asked why, and who carries the consequence if the answer is poor. An agent can be asked why, and a good one will answer, but it cannot carry the consequence. No auditor, board or court can hold it to account.

So an agent should hold no approval authority of its own. It can carry out an approval that a named person has already given, in the form of a rule. Its row in the matrix reads: no authority of its own; applies the rules listed, each signed by an owner.

Write the rule the way you would write an approval

If the agent's yes is a person's yes given in advance, the rule has to carry what that person would have checked. Five fields cover most of it: the owner, named as a person rather than a team; the kind of item the rule covers, such as invoices matched to an approved order within an agreed price tolerance; a limit per item; a limit per period; and a review date.

A matrix built for people seldom puts a period limit on each approver, because a person approving invoices is slowed down by the work itself. An agent is not, so the risk moves from the size of one item to the volume of many: thousands of small approvals in a month, each under the line, or one large purchase split into pieces that each clear it. A period limit, on value and on count, per supplier as well as in total, caps what the owner signed for, and the owner reviews the total on a stated date.

The review date exists because owners leave and agents are replaced. A rule without one stays in force after everyone who understood it has moved on.

What no rule can approve in advance

Some approvals cannot be given ahead of time at any amount, because the risk sits in the change rather than in the value. The clearest is a change to who gets paid: a new supplier, or a changed bank account for an existing one. A request to change a supplier's bank details is a common route for payment fraud, and it usually arrives looking routine. A rule can cover paying an existing supplier to its verified account. A change to that account needs a person who sees the specific request and confirms it through a channel the requester does not control.

Two more follow from the same principle. An agent never changes the rules it works under, its own limits included, because that would be approving its own authority. And an agent never approves work it prepared, which is the same separation of duties finance already requires of people.

The row to add

I would add one row to the matrix before the first agent clears its first item: “AI agent. Authority of its own: none. Applies only the rules listed below, each covering a stated kind of item, with a named owner, an item limit, a period limit on value and count, and a review date. May not approve a new payee or a change to a payee's bank details, changes to its own rules, or work it prepared.”

Then one thing has to be true before the owner signs each rule: the owner would approve every item the agent will clear under it, if they saw each one on its own. If that is not true, the rule is too wide, and the agent will approve, on the owner's behalf, items the owner would have stopped.
