Zero Exceptions Is Not a Clean Result

An adoption number counts whether people used the tool. It cannot see whether the tool did the work. Four questions that can, and not one of them requires a new dashboard.

Ashwin Kumar · 24 August 2026 · Companion to "The failures that pass review"

A reconciliation with no exceptions is not clean. It is untested. Every finance team knows that about a control. I have not seen anyone apply it to the number a function reports for AI.

Here is the problem. An adoption number counts whether people used the tool. It cannot see whether the tool did the work. Those are two different things, and the number reads the same for both.

Start with my own number, because it fails first. I have said, on my own unmeasured read, that my output moved by roughly three times. That is an impression. It has no instrumented basis. It would not survive the four questions below. I am the first case of this argument, not the exception to it.

Nobody in this story is cheating

Mandate a tool. Measure adoption, because adoption is the cheap thing to measure. Now stand where your team stands.

Somebody does not yet trust the output, and they are accountable for the deliverable.

So they do the work the way they know works, and they run the tool alongside it. The mandate is satisfied. The number moves. The number cannot see which of those two things happened.

That is not dishonesty, and I am not alleging it happens anywhere in particular. It is what any metric aimed at the wrong object will produce. Finance should expect it. The failure sits in the measurement design. It says nothing about the people being measured.

It persists because trust does not transfer. I trust a workflow I built because I watched it fail and then fixed it. I know where it lies to me. That knowledge is invisible to everybody else. They see a tool that writes confident prose, and no map of where it breaks. Until they have their own map, working the way they already trust is the responsible choice.

You cannot hand someone your calibration. Everybody earns their own, by watching the same tool fail in the same ways.

This is also how I came to the argument. I have been doing this daily for two years. Two years is long enough that if practice were the missing input for me, practice would have supplied it. That does not tell me what the answer is, and I want to be precise about how little it establishes. What it did was stop me looking at effort, and start me looking at what my own number was actually counting.

Four questions

Each one can be started this week. None needs a new system, and none reads anybody's private working record. That last part is deliberate. Reading somebody's version history, their session timestamps or their prompt archive is surveillance. A measurement aimed at a person is what caused the problem.

Each question has an innocent explanation that defeats it, and I have written it in. One question alone is a reason to look closer. It is never a finding.

All four can also be prepared for by anybody who knows they are coming, this essay included. So none of them is a control. The fourth is the only one I would put in a policy, because it does not depend on an answer.

01
Can they tell you where the tool fails on their own work?

Ask anybody who reports high use one question: where does this thing lie to you, on your deliverable? Somebody who genuinely depends on it answers in seconds, concretely, with an example and a workaround. Somebody who has been working alongside it gives you a general answer about hallucination. They will tell you it invents a plausible driver when the variance is small, or that it treats a one-off as a run rate unless you stop it. This takes five minutes and tells you more than any dashboard. It is also the easiest of the four to rehearse, so it cannot stand alone.

Innocent explanation: a genuinely careful user on a narrow, well-bounded task may honestly have met few failures. Ask what they check before they trust it instead. And a failure mode somebody has published is one anybody can recite, the two above included. Listen for their example, not the category.

02
Where did the saved hours go?

A real capacity gain has a destination. Something got absorbed at flat headcount, an analysis that never used to fit now happens, a weekend came back, a draft that used to land on Friday lands on Wednesday. If the number moved and nobody can name what the recovered time was spent on, the saving has not reached the process. Note what this question does not ask about: the close date and the filing date. Those are fixed externally and will not move however fast drafting gets, which is why they are a bad place to look.

Innocent explanation: growth can absorb the gain, so the same team carries more without anybody calling it a saving. That is real. It should still be nameable.

03
Did anything more arrive at the review step?

If drafting genuinely collapsed, more drafts should reach review, and reach it earlier. Review is the constraint that binds. Read this one carefully, though, because it cuts both ways. If your reviewers are already saturated, volume cannot rise however fast drafting gets. Flat volume is then what a real gain looks like, and not evidence against one.

Innocent explanation: the same volume arriving in better shape is a genuine gain, and it shows up as less rework. Ask the reviewers whether what they receive has got better, not only whether there is more of it.

04
Is the first pass part of the working paper?

Not as a retrospective challenge, which retention policies defeat and which turns into a hunt. As a standing convention, from now: the tool's first pass is filed with the deliverable, the same way a supporting schedule is. The cost is one filed attachment per deliverable, which is the cheapest item on this list and the only one needing no new system. It also makes the question routine instead of adversarial. After a quarter you can see how much of a first pass survives into what ships. Rising survival is trust being built. Near zero, sustained, tells you the draft is decoration.

Innocent explanation: an expert may argue with the tool and write the prose themselves. Low survival with heavy genuine use is a real pattern, so read this as a trend and not a threshold. The filed pass is also handed over by the person, so it can be curated. Read the trend, never one deliverable.

What to measure instead

Each of these replaces a count of activity with evidence that a process changed.

Exception rate
Corrections and refusals logged per deliverable. This one you have to institute, because I have not seen it asked for. An empty log usually means nobody was ever asked, not that nothing went wrong. The target is not zero. It is non-zero and stable, because that is what a tested control looks like. That is also why a self-reported log survives the obvious objection: when zero is the failing answer, nobody has an incentive to report clean.
Survival rate
How much of the tool's first pass reaches the final artifact. Same caveat as question four, since the artifact is handed over by the person. Read it as a trend across a quarter, per deliverable type, never as a judgement on a person.
Cycle time
Elapsed time from trigger to signed deliverable. A real gain shortens the internal path even when the external date is fixed.
Review load
Drafts entering review against drafts cleared. The binding constraint, and the thing an adoption number misses.
Recovery time
From a wrong output being caught to the correction being filed where the next person will read it. The only one that compounds.

The move

Stop asking how much the tool is used and start asking what it changed. Institute the exception log, then ask for it before the adoption report. Make the first pass part of the working paper. Point the measurement at the process, because a person can always satisfy a number aimed at them, and a process cannot pretend.

I do not have this solved. What I have is a number I no longer trust and four questions that tell me why, which is a better position than a number I believed.

If you have an AI productivity figure you would defend to your board, can you produce the exception log behind it? If you lead finance or operations and you are trying to tell a real gain from a reported one, that is the conversation I want to have.
