Essay · Finance, run on AI

What never goes into the model

An AI-run finance function earns the right to operate by deciding, in advance and in writing, what the model is never allowed to see. That line is drawn before there is a deadline to test it.

Ashwin Kumar · 21 September 2026 · On running a finance function on AI

A finance function running on AI is judged on what it produces. It earns the right to operate on something that never appears in the output: the decision about what the model is never allowed to touch. Draw that boundary badly and nothing downstream matters, because the fastest, best-sourced answer in the world is worthless if producing it meant handing a confidential thing to a system you do not control.

That boundary comes before the prompt, before the model, before the answer. Everything else the function does is built on top of it.

Decided once, before the first prompt

Most people treat data safety as a judgment made in the moment. They look at the thing they are about to paste and ask whether it feels sensitive. That method fails under deadline pressure, which is exactly when the sensitive thing is most tempting to paste, because pasting it is the fastest way to the answer.

So the boundary cannot live in your judgment on the day. It has to be set in advance and written down, once, as policy. On any given Tuesday you are not deciding what is safe. You are obeying a rule you wrote on a calm Monday, when nothing was on fire and no one was waiting.

A boundary decided under deadline is not a boundary. It is a coin flip with your reputation on the table.

Categories, never the item

There is a trap in writing the policy down. The moment you list the specific things the model may not see, you have described the specific things that exist. A list of exclusions is a map of the vault. Name what is hidden and you have told the reader precisely where to look.

So the policy is written one level up, in categories. Anything that identifies a customer, a counterparty, or a person. Anything tied to a live deal or a negotiation in progress. Anything that is not already public or already cleared for release. The category is the unit. The specific instances stay out of the policy document for the same reason they stay out of the model.

The boundary has to be abstract to be safe. A concrete boundary leaks the very thing it is meant to protect.

The test that decides

Every prompt faces one question. Would I be comfortable if this exact prompt appeared, word for word, in the vendor’s training logs, readable by a stranger a year from now?

If the answer is no, the prompt does not go in that form. That call is made by policy, not by mood. The test does not soften at 11pm the night before a board meeting, and it does not tighten when an auditor is in the building. It reads the same at every hour, which is the only reason it is worth anything.

The governed lane versus the open tools

Not every AI surface is the same, and the boundary is what tells them apart. There is a governed lane: the environment the company has contracted for, where data is not retained, is not used for training, and is covered by an agreement the legal team has actually read. Then there are the consumer tools anyone can open in a browser, where the terms are whatever the vendor decided this quarter.

Confidential work goes in the governed lane or it does not happen. The open tools are for public information, general reasoning, the things you would already put on a slide. The wall between the two is drawn once, in advance, so that no one is deciding lane membership while the clock runs.

One request, walked through

This stays abstract until a tempting case arrives. Here is the kind that shows up on a normal Tuesday.

The request · a summary due in an hour

A colleague forwards a supplier contract and asks for a one-page read on the commercial risk before a call that starts in sixty minutes. The document names the supplier, the pricing, the termination terms, all of it. The fast move is obvious: paste the whole contract into a tool, ask for the summary, be finished in four minutes. Every incentive in the moment points at pasting it.

The boundary stops that move before it starts, and it does not stop it by asking me to weigh anything. The document is covered by two categories at once. It identifies a counterparty, and it is deal-related, and neither is cleared for release. The question is never "should I paste this." That question was answered on the calm Monday. The document does not enter any ungoverned surface.

What the discipline does instead is separate the structure of the problem from its substance. The structure of a commercial-risk review is general knowledge: what to check in a termination clause, how to read a liability cap, where auto-renewal traps tend to hide, which indemnities move the risk. I can ask the model every bit of that without the model ever seeing this contract.

I get a checklist and a set of questions back. Then I apply them to the actual document myself, or inside the governed lane where the agreement permits the file to go. The confidential substance never crosses the wall. The reasoning I needed crossed freely. The summary still gets written inside the hour, and nothing that identifies the counterparty ever touched a system I do not control.

Why this comes before everything else

The boundary is a control on the input. It decides what is allowed in. There is a separate discipline that runs on the output and decides what the model is allowed to say: the gates that every number, name, and claim passes on the way out, which I have written about separately.

Both matter, and the order is not negotiable. A gate on the way out does nothing about a confidential file that already went in. Verification cleans the answer. The boundary decides whether the question should ever have been asked of that system at all.

Across the function, the boundary is the one control that is not workflow-specific. Each workflow carries its own logic and its own checks. All of them share the same wall. That is why the supplier contract never got pasted: the fast part of the work never includes the decision about what the model sees. That decision was made slowly, once, on purpose.

The boundary is what makes an AI finance function allowed to exist at all. Every claim about speed and scale is worth exactly nothing until you can say, without checking your mood, that the confidential thing never crosses the wall.

Write the boundary down before you need it. One page, three categories, decided on a quiet afternoon. It will be the cheapest control you ever ship, and the only one that has to exist before the first prompt does.

Where does your team draw the line on what the model sees — in a written policy, or in whoever happens to be holding the deadline? If you lead finance or operations and you are moving AI from pilot to real cadence, that is exactly the conversation I want to have.
