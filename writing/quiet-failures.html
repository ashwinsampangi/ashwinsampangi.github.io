The failures that pass review

The dangerous failures look more finished than they are. Finish is the disguise.

Ashwin Kumar · 17 August 2026 · Part of the series that begins with "The five levels"

Finance work rarely fails loudly, whoever drafted it. A broken formula throws an error, a missing file will not open, and both get fixed inside the hour because they announce themselves. The failures that cost you are the quiet ones. The output looks more finished than it is, and a clean draft reads as a checked draft, so the reviewer's eye rewards a polish it never earned.

Over two years moving a global finance function onto AI, the loud failures stopped mattering within weeks. Once the work ran at volume, across 45+ workflows and the whole function rather than one desk, the errors that survived that scale were never the obvious ones.

They were the quiet ones. None of the five is new — a capable junior can produce every one of them; the model just produces them faster, cleaner, and in higher volume. Each hides behind something that normally signals quality, which is precisely why the eye slides past it. Here are five I have learned to catch. Every vignette below is drawn from the shape of the miss rather than from any real number, period, or name.

The precision that was never there

Phantom precision

An analyst asks the model to summarize a cost line. The only source is one rounded figure, a budget of forty thousand. The draft returns 40,127.63. Nobody typed those cents, and no calculation produced them. Trained on millions of tables where real figures carry decimals, the model supplies decimals because that is what a real number looks like to it.

The reviewer sees two decimal places and reads diligence. Precision is the visual grammar of a checked number, so the eye grants it a trust it has not verified. A round forty thousand looks like an estimate. The figure 40,127.63 looks like a fact, and it is the same number wearing a lab coat.

The tell is precision that exceeds its source. When a figure is finer than any input it could have come from, the extra digits were manufactured. Trace one number back to its cell. If the source was round and the output is not, you have found invention, not calculation.

Last quarter, wearing this quarter's clothes

The laundered number

A reviewer asks the model to convert this quarter's euro figures into dollars. The model applies an exchange rate it found sitting in the working file and returns a clean set of converted numbers. The rate is real, but it is last quarter's, carried forward and never updated. Nowhere does the draft say as of when the rate holds. Every converted figure is plausible, internally consistent, and wrong by exactly the drift in the rate since.

A rate stated without a date reads as a rate stated as of now. The absence of an as-of date does not look like missing information. It looks like currency. And because the conversion is arithmetically sound, every figure it produces inherits the same quiet error without a single one of them looking wrong.

The tell is a figure, or a rate behind it, with no as-of date attached. Every number describes a moment, and a converted number describes two: the figure and the rate that moved it. If the draft cannot say which moment each one holds, it does not know whether the number is current, and neither do you. Ask for the date before you accept the figure.

Polish makes a wrong number worse

The polished error

An inherited model carries a growth rate that was mistyped two versions ago. The analyst asks the model to write the narrative around it. The prose comes back clean: the rate is set in a well-built sentence, framed against context, given a plausible cause. The error is now beautifully presented. Every round of refinement made it read more credible, and not one round touched whether it was true.

Here is the part that runs against instinct, and it is the whole point of this essay. We treat editing as a filter that strains out error. On an unverified number it does the opposite. A rough wrong figure invites a second look, because its roughness is a warning label. A polished wrong figure reads as finished, and finish suppresses the second look. Refinement did not reduce the risk in that number; it removed the one signal that might have caught it.

A well-written wrong number reads more credible than a rough one. That is why editing an unverified figure makes it more dangerous, not less.

The tell is your own comfort. When a number feels more trustworthy after it has been written up, ask what actually changed. If the answer is the prose and not the verification, the comfort is decoration. Polish and truth are set by two different steps, and only one of them was done. Verify the figure at its source before you let anyone make it read well.

The sentence nobody said

The composite quote

An analyst feeds the model three messages from a colleague and asks for a summary of their position. The draft returns a single crisp quotation inside quotation marks. It captures the gist. The person never said it. The model stitched three separate statements, made across three different days for three different reasons, into one sentence and dressed it in quotes.

Quotation marks are a claim of verbatim record. The reader sees them and stops treating the words as summary, because now they read as testimony. The tighter and more quotable the composite, the more authoritative it sounds, and the further it has drifted from anything a single person actually said.

The tell is a quotation you cannot trace to one source. A real quote has one origin: one message, one date, one speaker. If the words were assembled from three places, they are a paraphrase in a costume. Attribute it to the summary, or find the one line that was genuinely said and quote only that.

Two numbers that do not belong in the same subtraction

The basis mismatch

A reviewer wants the gap between plan and actual. The model finds a plan figure and an actual figure, subtracts one from the other, and presents the difference as a variance. The plan was built gross and the actual was reported net, or one included a category the other excluded. The two numbers sit on different definitions. The subtraction is real arithmetic on two things that were never the same measurement, and it is presented as a gap that means something.

Subtraction looks objective. A minus sign carries no visible doubt, so a number that survives it reads as settled. The reviewer checks the arithmetic, finds it correct, and moves on, never asking whether the two inputs measured the same thing. The error sits upstream of the math, where nobody is looking.

The tell is a comparison whose two sides were never reconciled to the same basis. Before you subtract, confirm that both figures are gross or both net, cover the same periods, the same entities, the same categories. If you cannot confirm the basis matches, the difference is noise with a decimal point.

What they have in common

Notice the pattern across all five. Not one is a computational error. Every one is a presentation error, where the draft looks more finished than its contents justify, and finish is exactly what a reviewer is trained to reward. That is why they are quiet. They pass the glance.

You cannot fix this by reading harder. Vigilance does not scale, and a tired reviewer at the end of a long day will slide past all five. The answer is to stop relying on the eye at all. Each tell above is a question that can be asked the same way every time, which means it can be encoded into a check the system runs before you ever see the draft.

This is what a standing verification layer is for. The four gates I run every number through — a source on every figure, a recency check with an as-of date, internal consistency, and a citation on every quote — each exist because one of these quiet failures once reached someone it should not have. Phantom precision is caught by the source gate. The laundered number is caught by recency. The composite quote is caught by the citation gate. The basis mismatch is caught by internal consistency, which is the gate that asks whether two numbers in the same sentence were ever the same measurement. The polished error is the one no gate catches, and that is why it sits in the middle of the five. Its tell is your own comfort, and comfort cannot be encoded. What can be encoded is the order: verification runs before the writing-up, never after, so no figure is ever made to read well before anyone has checked whether it is true. Four gates and a sequence. The gates catch four of the five; the sequence catches the fifth. The tells are not reminders I hope to recall in the moment; they are checks that run whether I remember them or not.

The mechanism for turning a single tell into a permanent check is the one I have written about as case law. The first time a quiet failure gets through, you write the tell down, dated and pointing at the miss that caused it, and you make the system read it before the next run. The failure is allowed to happen once. It is not allowed to happen quietly a second time.

The uncomfortable truth underneath all five is that AI-drafted work fails most where it looks best. Polish is evidence of polish, and nothing more. The teams that get real value from these tools are the ones that stopped trusting finish and started encoding the checks that finish was quietly hiding.

Where does your team catch its quiet failures — in a standing check, or in whoever happens to be paying attention that day? If numbers cross your desk on their way to people who act on them, that is a conversation worth having.
