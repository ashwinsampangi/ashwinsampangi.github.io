Price the exit before you sign

An approval matrix reads what a commitment costs if it goes to plan. The scrutiny it deserves also depends on what it costs to get out, and that cost is easiest to change before signature.

Ashwin Kumar · 19 October 2026

Many approval matrices ask one question: how much? Under one threshold a manager signs, above the next a function head, above the last the CEO or the board. It is easy to run and audit, and it reads one of the two numbers that matter.

Face value is the first: the amount on the request, the cost if the commitment runs as planned. The other is the cost if it does not, the price of getting out once you know it was a mistake. For a CEO or a board, the commitments that are expensive to leave decide how fast the company can change direction when a plan is wrong.

Two requests on the same desk

The first is a one-off equipment purchase just above a manager's authority. If the project it serves is cancelled, the equipment is redeployed or resold at a loss.

The second is an outsourcing contract whose expected annual spend, the figure on the request, is about a quarter of that. It is signed for three years with a minimum annual volume close to that spend, paid whether or not the work arrives, renews automatically unless notice is served ninety days before term-end, and comes with a transition that moves an in-house process to the provider.

The matrix sends the first request higher. Six months in, the first unwinds with one decision and a write-down. Leaving the second means paying out two and a half years of minimums and rebuilding the process in-house, or moving it a second time. The smaller request carried the larger exit cost, and a lower approver signed it.

What the exit costs

The exit cost has four parts. Contractual: the minimum payments still owed when you stop, plus any termination fee. Build: setup and transition work that is worth nothing once the commitment ends. Switching: the move to whatever replaces it, including a period of running both.

The fourth part, organisational, shows on a hire: reversing one means notice pay or severance and a second search. The salary line on the requisition carries none of that.

Put the exit cost on the request

I would add a second number to every spend request, in its own field: the exit cost. Finance sets a decision date for each category of spend, the point by which you would know whether the commitment is working: for an outsourced process, the first quarter after transition; for a hire, the end of probation. The exit cost is the total cost of leaving at the first date you could stop after it. The requester fills in the figure, and finance checks the contractual part against the contract before the request reaches an approver.

Then route approval on the higher of the two, using the tiers you already have. Exit cost is paid only if the call was wrong, so it sizes the downside and leaves the odds to the approver. A request with a modest face value and a large exit cost climbs to the approver who owns that exit.

That does not send every multi-year contract to the top. The exit cost counts only what cannot be recovered, and only to the first date you could stop after the decision date, so a three-year deal you can break after year one carries only the minimums left to the end of year one.

The second number also earns a shortcut. A request whose exit cost is close to zero, cancellable monthly with nothing built around it, stays at its face-value tier but skips the full business case, provided finance has read the cancellation term in the contract.

The same field moves the negotiation to before signature. A requester who has to write down the cost of leaving has a reason to go back to the vendor and ask for a termination-for-convenience clause, a shorter minimum term, or a pilot priced separately from the full transition. Each one lowers the exit cost, and with it the tier the request lands in. After signature, the same clause has to be bought back.

What a delegation policy already covers

A delegation policy can come close in two ways. Some set thresholds on total contract value, which captures a multi-year term. It still assumes the contract runs to term, so it overstates a three-year deal you can cancel on thirty days' notice and ignores every cost that sits outside the contract.

Others send long or auto-renewing contracts to legal or procurement review. Both can read the termination terms and push to improve them. Whether to accept the price those terms set is a budget call, for the approver who owns the budget.

Where the estimate is soft

Exit costs are estimates, and requesters will shade them down. The contractual part is read off the document, so the floor is a fact. The rest is no softer than the benefits case on the same request, which approvers already accept and which is often not revisited. The exit cost is tested: when a commitment is unwound, finance files the actual cost of leaving next to the number its requester wrote at approval.

Record the auto-renewal notice date next to the exit cost, and send the renewal back through approval sixty days before that notice date. The notice date is the last day the commitment still ends where the approver signed it to end. The day after, it runs for a full new term.
