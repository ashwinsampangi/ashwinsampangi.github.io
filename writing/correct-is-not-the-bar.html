Correct Is Not the Bar

A review that only asks whether the work is right will pass work that changes nothing. Catching that is not a matter of more discipline.

Ashwin Kumar · 7 September 2026

Last week I finished an essay, had it reviewed, and threw it away. Nothing in it was wrong. That is the part worth writing about.

The piece was accurate. Every claim was sourced or labelled. It had been through a check for confidentiality, for numbers with no basis, for the tics that make writing sound machine-made. It cleared all of that. Then a red-team pass I run with a model, in a context that had never seen me write the thing, pointed out that most of the argument repeated what I had already published, in different words. The essay was correct and it was pointless. I threw it away and started again.

My review had tested one property. Whether the piece was worth reading was not among the things it looked at.

The check almost every review runs

Ask what your own review actually tests. In my experience it comes down to correctness. Do the numbers tie, is the logic sound, is anything overstated. That question is necessary, and it is the whole of most review processes I have seen, including my own until last week.

The remedy is not new, and I want to be clear about that before I claim anything. Every analyst is taught to ask what follows from the page. I have never seen it written down as a control, with a stated pass and fail, which is why it gets asked in conversation and never in review.

This is not a writing problem

A variance memo arrives. The numbers tie. The commentary is careful. The drivers named are the real drivers. It passes review, goes into the pack, and the reader learns nothing they did not already know from the same page last quarter. That memo is accurate, and it is waste. It consumed a preparer, a reviewer and a slot in front of a board, and it moved no decision.

I have approved pages like that. The memo above is a composite, and the pattern is the point. The review asked whether the numbers were right. Nobody asked whether the page said anything new.

Correctness is the floor. Almost every review I have run measures the floor and then stops.

Two questions worth adding

The first is the one my own review missed, and it is the one I would put in a policy.

Redundancy
Has this already been said, by us, in the last few cycles? Not the numbers, which are always new. The argument, the framing, the conclusion.
Delta
What changed since the last version of this same deliverable, beyond the figures? It fails if the only thing that changed is the numbers.

Both are cheap to ask. Only one of them is ever asked, and the reason is more interesting than a lack of rigour.

Why redundancy survives review

Three things protect it, and two of them are legitimate.

A reviewer needs standing to reject work that is correct. There is no procedural home for the sentence this is accurate and it adds nothing, and saying it out loud attacks a colleague's judgment rather than a number, which is a much harder thing to do in a review meeting.

Some repetition is also deliberate. A board reading the same page every quarter is reading a trend, and comparability is often the deliverable. A director who missed the last two calls needs the page that everybody else has already seen.

Neither of those is an argument for never asking. Both are arguments for asking against a record, so the answer is a fact. The record also separates the two: repetition that was decided in advance is declared in the record, and everything else is discovered in review.

Which brings me to the third thing, and the only one that is nobody's fault. Answering the question requires knowing what was already said, and that is memory rather than judgment. My own review missed the repetition in my essay even though everything I had published sat on one indexed page. The knowledge was addressable. Nothing in the review addressed it.

That is the same mechanism that lets a variance commentary repeat itself for four quarters running. Nobody compares this quarter's page against the last four. Each one is reviewed on its own, and on its own every one of them passes.

So the redundancy test needs a record to exist before anybody can ask it. Which makes this an operating-model problem rather than a discipline problem, and it is fixable with something dull: a file of what has already been argued, kept where the reviewer already looks.

I built that file the same week. It lists what each published piece already argued, and it now sits in front of every draft. It is the least sophisticated thing I have shipped this year.

The second failure was the better one

The replacement essay had its own problem.

I ran a check on it for a stylistic tic and reported that the tic had fallen from twenty-three instances to seven. It had not. I had counted four specific phrasings, and the tic had simply moved to other phrasings. The count was honest. The method was wrong.

The register I had built the week before would not have caught this. It records what I argued, not how I measured. One failure bought me one control, and the next failure was already outside it.

I lost a week of work to a review that asked only whether I was right. At that price it was cheap. The expensive version is a finance function producing accurate, well-reviewed, entirely redundant analysis for years, with no single page ever failing an audit.

What does the reader of your last board page do differently because of it? If you cannot answer that, the page passed review and failed its job. If you lead finance or operations and you are trying to make review test for more than correctness, that is the conversation I want to have.
