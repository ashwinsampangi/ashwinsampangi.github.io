The reviewer who always agrees

Ashwin Kumar · 28 September 2026

A close package once reached me with every schedule initialed and not one challenge raised. It cleared review in two days. A quarter later, one of those schedules had to be reworked because a figure in it was wrong. The review had happened in form. It found nothing because nobody had built it to find anything.

Agreement should be the result of analysis. When it arrives before the analysis has been done, something in the process is broken, even though a clean file looks like the opposite of a problem.

The output of a review that works is not a stack of sign-offs. It is findings, pushback, and now and then an objection that gets overruled after a real argument. An approval stream with zero dissent over months is a red flag with a smiley face on it.

This holds whether the reviewer is a person or a machine. A human reviewer who wants to be agreeable fails in the same direction as a checking system tuned to confirm rather than test. Both hand back a yes, because a yes is what the design quietly rewards.

The drift toward yes is rarely laziness. It is incentive. Objecting carries a social cost that agreement does not; the person who raises the hard question slows the meeting, while the reviewer who passes the package is thanked for being quick. Machines drift the same way for their own reason. A system trained to be helpful reads approval as the helpful answer and a plain draft as a chance to encourage. In each case the reviewer optimizes for the wrong thing.

In a close, this shows up as a sign-off culture. Initials accumulate on schedules faster than anyone could have read them, and the speed gets mistaken for control. A reviewer who returns a package with questions becomes, quietly, the bottleneck. Once challenge is treated as friction, the review has already stopped working. It produces paperwork instead of assurance, and the paperwork is what gets filed.

A small example from outside the ledger made the shape of it clear. I set up an automated assistant to review my drafts before they went out. It kept returning encouraging summaries: well structured, logic sound, a little polish suggested. Reassuring, and after a while I stopped reading past the first line.

So I gave it a draft I had broken on purpose, with a real error planted in the middle, the kind a careful reader catches. It came back praised. The structure was admired. The error went through untouched.

The fix was not a better model. It was a different instruction. I told it to open every review by stating the single strongest objection to the work, and to treat a clean pass as a result that itself needed explaining rather than a reason to relax. Findings appeared on the next run. Several were real, and one would have been awkward in front of an audience.

The takeaway

I now measure any review layer by findings per cycle. Throughput tells you the reviewer is busy. Findings tell you the reviewer is working. The obvious way to game that measure is to invent objections, so count only the findings that survive being argued back. The mirror failure, a control that blocks correctly and then gets overridden anyway, is a different problem and not this one.

If a person or a system has agreed with everything for a quarter, treat that as a failure signal rather than a comfort. Rewrite the brief so that surfacing the strongest objection is the job, and an approval has to be earned against it.

Run this on your automated reviewer this week. Take a document you have already finished, change one figure in the middle so that it contradicts another figure on the same page, and hand it to whatever tool reviews your work. A reviewer that is working opens by naming the contradiction. One that is broken will admire the structure and hand it back.
