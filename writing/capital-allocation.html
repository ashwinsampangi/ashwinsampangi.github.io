The second-best use of the money

Every funding ask is a choice against one specific alternative. One required question on the ask template names it and gives it an advocate.

Ashwin Kumar · 12 October 2026

A funding ask reaches the review with a sponsor, a model and a slide. In funding reviews generally, the project it would push out of the plan arrives with nothing, so nobody in the room argues for it, and the ask is judged against nothing.

At that table, the finance leader is usually the one person without a project of their own in the queue. That makes the job specific: make the second-best use of the same money and the same leaders' time visible, and price the two on one basis.

The hurdle rate prices a generic alternative

A hurdle rate is an opportunity cost compressed into one figure: what the capital could earn elsewhere at similar risk, plus any premium the company adds. It is the right screen for weak asks. In the decision itself it prices a generic alternative and discards what the real one carries: a name, an owner, and a claim on specific people.

An ask can clear the hurdle with room to spare and still be worse than the particular project it displaces. Ranking asks by return per dollar orders them on money and leaves out a constraint no budget relaxes: the same few people.

Opportunity cost and capital rationing are first-year corporate finance. What the textbooks leave to the room is the procedure that puts a named alternative, argued by its own owner and charged for the same people's weeks, in the same document as the ask.

The weeks the budget leaves out

Unlike money, a senior leader's quarter cannot be split across two launches without one of them losing. Say a second store format and a loyalty relaunch are both affordable and both need the same merchandising head for the same two quarters. Approve both and one of them gets its budget and none of the leader's time, because the person who has to run it is running the other one.

Keep the leader's weeks in weeks. Converting them to dollars at salary cost prices a scarce leader as if a replacement could be hired next month. List the named people and quarters on both asks; if the same name sits on both in the same quarter, the two cannot both run as planned, whatever the return per dollar says.

The displacement question

The mechanism is one required question on every funding ask above the review threshold: what does this displace? The sponsor fills in the ask; finance answers this question, and three rules keep the answer honest.

It names one specific thing. A project, a hire, a release, the expansion of something already running. “Other priorities” is not an answer, and neither is “nothing, this is incremental”: an incremental ask still names whose weeks it takes.

The displaced use gets its own advocate. Finance takes it from the ranked list, because a sponsor has every reason to name a weak alternative. In a budget cycle that is the best item just below the funding line. Mid-year, or on an unbudgeted ask, there is no list, and finance names the next thing the same people would have run. That item's owner argues its half. Finance then puts both halves on one basis: the same horizon, the same measure of return, the same treatment of one-time cost, and each side charged for the named people's weeks it would consume. Finance builds the displaced item's numbers itself, so its owner writes a paragraph and no model.

Check the horizon first. If the ask is modelled at its year-three steady state and the alternative is judged, without anyone saying so, on what it delivers this year, put both on the same horizon: the ranking can flip with no other number moving.

The decision is recorded as a pair. Funded A over B, on this basis, for this reason. A plan-versus-actual review tests a funded bet against its own plan. With the pair on record, A's actual result can be set against the case B's owner argued. An advocate wrote that case, so read it as a ceiling: an A that beats it settles the choice, and an A that falls short leaves it open. If B is funded a cycle later, its actuals show how far its owner's case ran ahead, and that sets the discount on the next ceiling.

What it costs

The question adds friction, on purpose. Two leaders whose plans would have collided mid-year now collide in the review, where the CEO can settle it with both cases in hand. Some sponsors will withdraw an ask rather than see it argued, on one page, against a colleague's named project. That withdrawal is information the room would not otherwise have had.

The practical objection is that real portfolios do not trade one for one. The alternative is a scatter of small things, and naming any one looks arbitrary. The scatter still has an order once a budget is set. Name the items below the funding line, in that order, until their cost matches the ask; the first carries the case, the rest are listed by name and amount. If nobody can say what sits just below the line, the ranking has not been done yet, and that is the first thing to fix.
