What a board actually reads on a variance page

The page is written for completeness and read for consequence. A director reads it by answering three questions, in order.

Ashwin Kumar · 5 October 2026

A variance page takes a team days to build. A director reads it in the time it takes to find one number. That is fair: a board member is holding several companies or a long agenda, and the page earns exactly as much attention as its first three lines deserve.

The full walk from plan to actual, every line reconciled and every movement footnoted, is the writer's proof of work. It has to exist, and it belongs behind the page for anyone who asks. The page itself is a triage note. It decides what happens next.

Watch a good director work through one and the reading order is nothing like the writing order. They are answering three questions, in sequence, and they stop as soon as the answers hold.

One: is the year still intact?

The month is the trigger for the page, and it is the least interesting number on it. A director does not carry the monthly phasing in their head. What they carry is the full-year commitment — the number management last asked them to believe. So the first thing the eye hunts for is the full-year line: after this month's miss or beat, does the year still hold?

If the commentary makes them derive that answer from a monthly table, it has already failed. The first sentence on the page should state it: the year holds, or the year is at risk by a stated amount, or the year improves. Everything after that sentence is support.

Two: is this timing or trajectory?

Every variance, or every part of one, is one of two animals. Timing reverses: a deal slipped a month, an invoice landed early. It says nothing about the shape of the business, and the honest treatment is one line plus a date by which it unwinds. Trajectory compounds: the win rate moved, or the cost base reset. That one changes this year, and then the next. The full-year column is where the two separate. When a variance is both, split it and size each part.

In my experience a director forgives a miss that is labelled correctly. What erodes trust is timing language wrapped around a trajectory problem, repeated until the problem becomes undeniable.

The page should declare which animal each material variance is, and the declaration is falsifiable: a timing claim carries the reversal date, and if the date passes without the reversal, the claim was wrong in public. That discipline is uncomfortable, and it is the reason the page builds credibility.

Three: does management know why?

The third question is about the team behind the number. A driver named with a direction (volume, price, mix, timing, rate) signals a function that understands its own machine. A reconciliation without a cause signals only that the function can add. And the most expensive sentence on any variance page is we are investigating, because it converts a finance question into a management-confidence question in front of exactly the wrong audience.

Knowing why needs a stated, checkable explanation: this driver, this direction, this evidence, and here is what we will see next month if we are right. Wrong-but-falsifiable beats vague, for the same reason the timing date does: it shows the team is willing to be tested.

Writing for the reading order

Once you accept the three questions, the craft rules write themselves. Open with the full-year consequence in one sentence. Label every material variance timing or trajectory, and attach the reversal date to every timing claim. Name the driver with a direction, and separate one-time effects from run-rate effects so the reader is never averaging two different futures. Say what changed since the last read, because the board met the prior version of this page and repetition without movement reads as drift. And if the page implies a decision, put the ask in the last line.

None of this depends on how the numbers were produced. A page assembled by hand and a page drafted by a machine fail the same way: by answering the writer's questions instead of the reader's.
