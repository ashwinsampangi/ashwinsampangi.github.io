What I Look For in a Finance Hire Now

When a clean draft costs seconds, the skill I hire for is no longer producing the work. It is judging whether the work is true.

Ashwin Kumar · 27 July 2026 · Companion to "The five levels"

For a long time, drafting was the job. A finance analyst earned the seat by producing — building the model, writing the variance note, turning a raw ledger into a page a manager could read. That skill is now close to free. Any competent tool produces a clean-looking draft in seconds. So the question I ask in an interview has changed, and most of the field has not caught up.

The scarce skill is no longer production. It is review judgment: the ability to look at a plausible, well-formatted output and know, quickly, whether it is true. Most finance interviewing still screens for the thing that got cheap.

A draft is nearly free now. Knowing whether it is true is the entire job.

Watch a typical finance interview and you still see the old test: a timed modeling case, a mental-math drill, a build-this-schedule exercise. All of it measures how fast someone produces. None of it measures whether they can catch a number that is quietly wrong. We are grading the sprint after the race moved indoors.

The disqualifier

There is one thing that ends an interview for me, and I test for it on purpose. I hand the candidate a variance summary that a tool produced — a page of clean prose explaining why gross margin improved on the prior year. It reads well. Every sentence lands.

Buried in it is one quiet flaw. The page sets this year's gross margin against last year's and narrates the improvement in detail. But the two figures do not sit on the same basis. This year's margin is net of a cost reclassification that moved a line out of cost of goods; last year's is not. Put both on the same definition and the improvement mostly disappears. The gain the page celebrates is an artifact of the basis change, not the business. I have seen this exact mistake more often than any other.

Then I watch. The candidate I want reads for a minute, stops, and asks whether the two years are measured the same way. "This margin — is last year restated for the reclass, or not? If one side is net of it and the other is not, this improvement is not real." They found the seam before they touched a word of the prose.

The candidate I do not want reads the same page and improves it. Tighter sentences, a cleaner opening line, a heading that was not there. They accepted the draft as true and made it read better. They polished a false statement until it shone.

That is the disqualifier. Missing the flaw once is forgivable; anyone can miss one. What ends it is the instinct: handed a clean artifact, they moved to trust and refine rather than to distrust and check. In a world where the machine produces the artifact, that instinct is the whole job. A reviewer who does not ask where a number came from is not reviewing anything.

What replaces the speed test

For a decade the entry test in finance was speed and accuracy in a spreadsheet — build the model faster, cleaner, with fewer errors. That skill still matters. It no longer sorts the good from the great, though, because the tool does the mechanical part now. Three things sort them instead.

1
Can they specify the work

Hand a strong candidate a vague instruction and watch them refuse it. A sloppy ask — "explain the margin decline" — produces confident garbage from any tool, because the tool fills the gaps you left with plausible invention. The skill is to specify: which margin, against which period, on what basis, for whose eye. Precision in the ask is a core finance skill now, and the trade used to treat it as clerical.

2
Can they write a standard

Ask them to turn a judgment they hold into a rule a junior could apply without them in the room. Most people who are good at the work cannot explain it; they operate on feel. The ones worth hiring can write the standard down — "a variance over five points needs a named driver and the data cut that proves it" — so the standard survives them and does not walk out at six o'clock.

3
Do they encode a correction

Give them a fix and see what they do with it. The weak version repairs the one instance and moves on. The strong version encodes it — writes the rule down, dated, so the same miss cannot recur next quarter. I have written about treating corrections as case law; the habit is rare, and it is the clearest signal that someone will compound rather than repeat.

What has not changed

None of this retires the fundamentals. If anything it raises their price.

Accounting judgment stays. You have to know what the number ought to be before the tool tells you what it is, or you cannot tell when the tool is lying. Skepticism stays — the reflex to disbelieve a clean answer is now the thing standing between you and a false story going to the board. And ownership stays hardest of all.

The machine has no license to lose. When the number is wrong in front of the board, the tool does not answer for it; the person who signed it does. Everything above is in service of that one fact. The human still owns the number, and no amount of speed changes who is accountable for it.

The uncomfortable part

There is an honest problem underneath all of this, and I would rather name it than pretend it away. Review judgment used to be a byproduct of years of production. You built a thousand models, and somewhere in the reps you learned to smell a wrong one. The production was the training. It came free with the grind.

Now the production is automated. So the training that used to arrive with it does not happen on its own. The reviewer's instinct I am screening for is exactly the thing the new workflow no longer manufactures. If a junior never builds the thousand models, where does the nose come from?

I have watched capable analysts stall at exactly this gap. They can run any workflow you hand them, and they cannot tell you which output to distrust, because nobody ever made them sit with a wrong answer long enough to learn its shape.

I do not have a clean answer. What I am sure of is that the path to senior judgment has to be designed on purpose now — deliberate reps, deliberate exposure to being wrong, deliberate time spent tracing a number back to its source when the tool would happily hand you the number and skip the lesson. It will not accumulate as a side effect any more, because the side effect is what got automated.

The plateau I described in the Level 3 trap is what bad hiring reproduces at scale: teams full of people who can operate the tool and cannot judge its output, because nobody built the judgment on purpose. Hire for the polish and you get a room that ships confident errors faster every quarter.

So here is the one-line screen worth stealing. Hand the candidate a clean, confident, AI-drafted page with a single quiet error in it, and say nothing except: "walk me through this." What they do in the next two minutes tells you more than an hour of case questions. The ones who reach for the source before they reach for the prose are the hire. Everyone else is auditioning for a job the machine already does.

The screen is the easy part. What I have not solved is how to manufacture the judgment it tests for in someone who never had to build the thousand models. If you have found a way, I would rather hear it than keep guessing.

If you are building or leading a finance team through this shift, that is exactly the conversation I want to have.
